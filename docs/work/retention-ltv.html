Retention and lifetime value: who should get the offer?

I've built season ticket retention and lifetime value models for a professional sports team, and done lifetime value work for iRobot's online store. The question that matters is who an offer actually changes, and that often isn't the customer most likely to leave.

Where I've done this

  • A professional sports team, on the agency side: built retention, lifetime value, and acquisition cost models, and helped drive the season ticket package, retention, and upsell strategy built on them.
  • iRobot: extended lifetime value analysis to the e-commerce business, alongside A/B and promotion testing.

Demo: a retention decision on made-up data

The real data stays private, so everything from here down is simulated: a subscription business with 30,000 customers who were randomly offered, or not offered, a $25 retention incentive. Some customers respond to the offer, some would stay or leave anyway, and a few become more likely to cancel when reminded.

  • Churn: a gradient boosting model for each arm of the test estimates how likely each customer is to stay, with and without the offer.
  • Lifetime value: those estimates turn into expected active months over 24 months, multiplied by each customer's monthly margin.
  • Decision: make the offer only when the lifetime value it adds is greater than what it costs.

I compared the rules on 20,000 new customers, across 20 simulated businesses. The three targeted rules each reached the same 25% of customers, so the only difference is who got the offer. Going after the customers most likely to leave lost money, because many of them leave anyway. Targeting by expected gain won every time.

Demo: how far to trust it

  • The model correctly decided whether to make an offer for 88% of customers.
  • Churn predictions were well calibrated, with an AUC of 0.67. Churn here is hard to predict customer by customer, which is realistic.
  • It overstated the gain: $6,249 promised per 1,000 customers against $3,067 delivered, since it favors customers whose gain it happens to overestimate. So I trust the ranking more than the dollar figure, and I'd roll out with a holdout to measure the real return.

This also relies on a past randomized test. Without one, a model can only tell you who's likely to leave, not who an offer would change.

The code

def months_with_offer(h_control, h_offer, offer_months=OFFER_MONTHS, horizon=HORIZON):
    #the offer changes churn for its first months, then behavior returns to normal
    first = expected_months(h_offer, offer_months)
    later = expected_months(h_control, horizon - offer_months)
    return first + (1 - h_offer) ** offer_months * later

def score(d, control, offer, cost):
    out = d.copy()
    out["p_control"] = control.predict_proba(d[FEATURES])[:, 1]
    out["p_offer"] = offer.predict_proba(d[FEATURES])[:, 1]
    h0, h1 = to_hazard(out.p_control), to_hazard(out.p_offer)
    out["ltv"] = out.margin * expected_months(h0)
    added = months_with_offer(h0, h1) - expected_months(h0)
    out["offer_value"] = out.margin * added - cost
    return out
